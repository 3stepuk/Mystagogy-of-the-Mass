The Mystagogy of the Mass

A walk through the Mass for anyone who has started coming, or is thinking about it.

What mystagogy means

The word means leading into the mystery. In the early Church it named the teaching given to the newly baptised in the weeks after Easter: now that you have been through it, here is what it was.

That is the right order, and it is the order this page follows. Nothing here is secret. It is simply the explanation that is rarely given, because everyone assumes you already know.

Liturgy is public work

The Greek word is leitourgia. It meant a public duty performed for the people, at a cost to the one performing it. Not a private feeling, and not a performance. In Christian use it kept that shape: the liturgy is the public work of the Church, offered to God on behalf of the world.

That is why Mass is not a service you attend as a spectator. There is no audience. There is a body, doing something together.

The shape: two halves

The Mass falls into two halves, and the split is ancient. First the Word, then the Sacrament. The first is a service of readings and preaching, whose pattern comes from the synagogue. The second is a thanksgiving meal, whose pattern comes from the Jewish blessing at table.

The earliest Christians did not invent a new religion from nothing. They took the worship they had known and saw it completed. That is why almost every part of the Mass can be traced to a Jewish source: the psalms, the readings, the blessing of bread and wine, the thanksgiving.

The first half: the Word

The Mass begins with the sign of the cross and a greeting. Then the Kyrie, which is Greek for Lord, have mercy, a remnant of the Greek the early Roman Church prayed in. On Sundays and feasts the Gloria follows, an ancient hymn of praise. Then the collect, a single prayer that gathers the whole assembly's intention into one sentence, and which the priest prays on everyone's behalf.

Then the readings. On Sunday there are three, with a psalm sung between them: a passage from the Old Testament, a psalm, a letter of the apostles, and then a passage from one of the Gospels, for which everyone stands. The homily opens the readings, then the Creed is said, then the prayers of the faithful, in which the Church prays for the world by name.

A word about the readings: they are not samples. The Church reads through the Scriptures in a cycle, so that over three years a congregation hears a very large part of the Bible read aloud. Come for a year and you will have heard more Scripture than most people read in a lifetime.

The second half: the Sacrament

The offerings are brought forward: bread and wine, and with them the work and the money of the people. The old word is prosphora, an offering. Nothing here is a purchase. It is the assembly's life, presented.

Then the Eucharistic Prayer, which is the centre of the Mass and the part most people do not know is there. It has a fixed shape. It begins with thanksgiving, the same movement as the Jewish blessing at table. It asks the Father to send the Holy Spirit on the gifts and on the people, which is called the epiclesis. It recounts the words of Christ at the Last Supper. Then comes the anamnesis.

The word that matters: anamnesis

At the Supper Christ said: do this in memory of me. The Greek is anamnesis, and it does not mean what memory usually means to us.

Our memory is a recollection of something absent. Anamnesis is a making-present of something real. It is closer to what a Jewish family does at Passover, where the telling makes the deliverance present to those at the table, and they are commanded to regard themselves as having come out of Egypt.

So the Mass is not a drama about a past event. It is the one sacrifice of Christ, made present here, now, for these people. The letter to the Hebrews says Christ offered himself once, and the Church has always held both things together: once, and present.

And the direction matters. The old phrase is a Godward memorial. The Church is not reminding itself. It is presenting the body and blood to the Father, in the power of the Spirit, and asking him to look on the offering of his Son.

Why sacramentum

The Latin word for mystery is sacramentum, and Tertullian, writing around the year 200, chose it deliberately. In Roman life a sacramentum was the oath a soldier swore on entering the army: a binding transfer of allegiance.

Baptism and the Eucharist, he argued, are that oath. They are not decorations on a belief. They are the act by which a person is transferred from one allegiance to another.

The altar, the priest, the people

Catholic teaching holds that the Mass is a sacrifice, and that the same Christ who offered himself on the cross is present and offered here. It holds that the bread and wine become his body and blood, not as a symbol but in reality, and that this is accomplished by the Spirit and the words of Christ, not by the priest's own power.

This is the point at which Christians have disagreed most deeply, and this page will not pretend otherwise. But it is worth knowing what the Catholic claim actually is, rather than the version of it that circulates in arguments. It is not that the Mass repeats the crucifixion. It is that the one crucifixion is made present.

What to do if you are new

Sit where you can see. Follow the words in the book or on the screen; nobody is testing you.

You are not expected to receive Communion. In Catholic practice, communion is for those who are in full communion with the Church and are properly disposed, and staying in your seat is not rudeness. It is honesty, and it is understood.

Stay afterwards if you can. The Mass ends with a dismissal whose Latin is ite, missa est, and that word missa is where the name Mass comes from. Go, you are sent. The point of the whole hour is that you leave.

This is a guide, not a rule book. The Church's own liturgical books govern the details, and they are more careful than any summary. If something here differs from what your parish does, the parish is right.
